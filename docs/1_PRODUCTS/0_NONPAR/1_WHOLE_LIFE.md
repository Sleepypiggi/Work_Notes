# **Whole Life Insurance**

## **Overview**

**Whole Life** insurance provides **virtually permanent coverage**, typically up to the 100th or 120th birthday of the insured, which is when the insurer expects that all individuals will die:

* Dies before age 100 or 120: Plan pays a **Death Benefit**
* Lives to age 100 or 120: Plan pays a **Maturity Benefit**
* Both are **mutually exclusive**, the policy will ALWAYS pay out a benefit

!!! Note

    Older policies have an upper age limit of 100 years, but due to increasing life expectancy, most insurers now use an upper limit of 120. The policy was designed with the intent that all individuals will die by this age. Thus, even an insured survives till then, the insurer will still make a benefit payout.

Another key difference is that whole life plans also accumulate **Cash Value**, which can be accessed via a full surrender of the policy. This allows whole life insurance to **double up as a form of savings vehicle**, which can be used to **cover certain obligations even while alive**.

Generally, whole life is typically suited for individuals who want to have **bundled product** of protection and savings, who also want to leave a **bequest for their beneficiaries**.

## **Premium Payment Term**

There are three possible premium payment patterns:

<center>

| **Regular Premium (RP)** |               **Limited Payment (LP)**               |    **Single Premium (SP)**     |
| :----------------------: | :--------------------------------------------------: | :----------------------------: |
|   Payable **for life**   | Payable for **fixed period** or till **certain age** | Payable only **once** at issue |
|    Lapse if not paid     |    Lapse if not paid; after which becomes paid up    |      Becomes **paid up**       |
|     Smallest premium     |             Significantly larger than RP             |        Largest premiums        |

</center>

The different payment patterns are meant to accomodate to different types of policyholders:

* **Regular Premium**: Individuals who have a **steady income stream**
* **Limited Payment**: Individuals who might **not have steady income** but sufficiently high to cover the higher amounts
* **Single Premium**: Individuals who have a **large amounts of excess cash**

!!! Tip

    There are two other implicit benefits:

    * **Regular Premium**: Allows individuals to prematurely end their policy and re-allocate the funds
    * **Limited Payment**: Allows individuals to make payments fully during working years, not having to worry about payments post retirement

The key advantage of whole life insurance is that the insured only needs to go through **medical underwriting once**, and the rates are locked in at the time of purchase. Regardless of how the health of the insured deteriorates, the policy will remain in-force at the same premium rate. This is in contrast to purchasing term insurance and then renewing it later in life, where the premium rates would increase substantially. Naturally, this privilege is **unfortunately only available to those who can afford the high cost of whole life insurance** at a young age.

## **Cash Value**

As mentioned above, whole life plans offer **Guaranteed Cash Values**, which are typically stated in the policy contract as a table split by **policy year**, **premium term** or other dimensions. There is typically **little to NO cash value** in the first few years of the policy (to allow the insurer to fully recoup the costs of writing the business) and builds up to be **equal to the death benefit** at the maturity age.

!!! Note

    Since the cash value can also be accessed via a surrender of the policy, it is sometimes referred to as the **Surrender Benefit**.

    Intuitively, the surrender benefit **CANNOT be higher** than the death benefit of the policy as the policyholder would then finacially gain more from surrendering the policy, which defeats the purpose of the policy.

Economically, cash values are essentially **part of the policy reserves** that is given back to the customer. Since the surrender benefit and death benefit are mutually exclusive, the insurer does not need to set-up a seperate reserve for the surrender benefit. The "growth" of the tabular surrender values is dependent on the **expected investment return of the portfolio** with other considerations for policyholder behaviour or competitiveness. 

### **Policy Loan**

Whole life plans also offer a **Policy Loan** facility where a loan can be **taken against the cash value** of the policy at the time. This allows policyholders to **access their cash values in times of need**, without having to fully surrender the policy. If any benefits are paid out before the loan is fully repaid, the outstanding loan balance will be offset from any benefit amount payable.

The outstanding loan balance accrues interest at a **fixed policy loan rate** (typically the insurer's expected return on the assets backing the portfolio), which is typically **higher than the cost of a loan from a bank**, which uses the prevailing the market rate at the time. If the outstanding loan balance exceeds the cash value, the policy will be automatically surrendered on the insurer's end to recoup the amount.

The insurer cannot offer loans at an attractive rate because the take-up rate would become too high, forcing them to keep their investments in relatively liquid assets, lowering their investment return. The facility is **not meant to compete with other sources of credit** but rather to provide the option if needed.

However, some argue that the prospect of having to pay regular premiums and the loan interest may be too much to handle, thus causing policyholders to abandaon the policy, resulting in lapses.

### **Buy Term Invest the Rest**

The intended purpose of cash values were to create **comprehensive financial product** with both an **insurance and savings component**, allowing it to compete with other savings products in the market. However, it **excels at neither** - it is too expensive as insurance and provides too little returns as a savings product.

Thus, modern financial advice has recommended NOT to combine the two components to achieve better results, known as a "Buy Term Invest the Rest" (BTIR) or "Buy Term Invest the Difference" (BTID)

* **Insurance Component**: Term Plan (typically lower premiums)
* **Investment Component**: Invest the premium savings (typically into a low cost index fund)
* Return from equity investments should typically **exceed the relatively low return on the insurer's portfolio** (largely corporate bonds)
* Better flexibility as the investments can be accessed at any time, without the need to affect the insurance component

Although this approach achieves better results, whole life remains commonplace in the market:

* Requires bearing the **investment risk** themselves; without the **right expertise**, the approach could lead to losses
* Requires discipline to save or routinely execute trades 

## **Non-Forfeiture Features**

In the past, when policyholders stop paying premiums (lapsing the policy), they get nothing in return, regardless of the amount of premiums paid, effectively forfeiting the entire value of the policy. Thus, the US introduced **strict non-forfeiture laws** to protect policyholders by ensuring insurers **policyholder's fair share** of the policy upon surrender. Although not all jurisdictions have such laws, the resulting features have become commonplace in the market.

There are a few common non-forfeiture practices:

* **Cash Surrender Benefit** - Policyholder value drawn out in hard cash
* **Extended Term Insurance** - Cash value used to purchase a **SP term policy with equivalent coverage**; converting to a shorter term policy
* **Reduced Paid Up** - Cash value used to purchase a **SP whole life policy** with lower coverage; converting to a smaller WL policy
* **Automatic Premium Loan** - *Automatically* takes a **policy loan to pay the required premiums**

The most intuitive explanation for policyholder equity would be the **accumulated value of premiums less the cost of the protection** for the duration. When valued using the same basis, this amount would be actuarially equivalent to the **prospective reserve** of the policy. This is why the reserve is commonly used as a benchmark

!!! Note

    Recall that for policy valuation, the two methods result in the same value:

    * **Prospective Method**: Present value of future outflows less inflows
    * **Retrospective Method**: Accumulated value of premiums less outflows
